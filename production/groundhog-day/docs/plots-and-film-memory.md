# Five films the loop could make

These are earlier treatments. The [current film](self-aware-town.md) opens up the whole town, allows the actor to recognize the movie and keeps the trip to Pittsburgh.

## 1. The 8:12 broadcast — the one I would shoot

**Tuesday, February 2, 1982.** A Pennsylvania town is waiting for its winter forecast. The station has one live channel, one field recorder and a glass partition between the studio and the booth. A visiting sound engineer has a single booking to record the audition that might get him out of town. Across the glass, a field recordist is trying to finish a piece of her own. The morning host has promised listeners their local announcements. All three want the same ninety seconds for different reasons.

Start on an ordinary sound check. Record the actual first exchange, right down to the sound of a dropped spoon. Repeat the sound and the first camera move when the day resets. A two-person job gives the engineer and the recordist a reason to meet. Someone has to set the gain in the booth while the other person is at the remote microphone. She can ask him, recruit the host, air something else or leave with her tape. He can help, do his audition, or give the host his time. He loses the slot if he spends it on someone else.

**The romantic question is whether he listens to the person in front of him when he already knows the tape by heart.** On one Tuesday she might choose to broadcast a recording. On another she might withdraw it. A world with a real mute switch, a source release and a finite recording slot lets that difference matter. Give her custody of her reel and a working talkback button. His learning can change what *he* gives up. Her decision controls whether her voice goes on air. A final minute of deliberately empty airtime could mean more than a speech, if the run earns the silence.

Keep the folded cue slip under the recorder lid. The worn rewind control takes the place of the watch. Put it on the station’s clock reel, physically separate from any recording she owns. The mechanism supplies two repeats and the wipe. The engineer chooses any subsequent rewind. Let a red tally lamp, reels moving in opposite directions and an audition tape still blank carry the ending visually. The credits can put the original writing event beside the rediscovered slip. The studio, winter crowd sound and warm diner light keep the small-town familiarity. A sound engineer’s trade gives the repetition a purpose unique to this story.

**What the rehearsal says.** Across 36 radio work opportunities, the cast issued three invitations. The engineer accepted one, from the host. The field recordist acquired no relationship bonus. That is enough to demonstrate that the system can favor a different person. It also identifies the next scene to audition. Give each character a small task they already chose and an independently attractive reason to work with somebody else. Vary the cost of saying yes. A free half-minute to hold a mic is plausible early in a relationship. Sacrificing an entire audition works better as a late test. [Working relationships in the rehearsal](rehearsal.md)

## 2. One Print Left

A seaside portrait kiosk opens on the last day of the pier. The photographer, the woman who runs the darkroom and the pier caretaker have one sheet of good paper left. She wants to choose how the pier is remembered. The caretaker promised a child a copy. The photographer has an unsigned photograph that nobody remembers taking, and a competition entry due on the last post.

Here memory comes in two stages. The exposure survives only as a latent image. Developing reveals the previous photographer’s choices but uses chemicals needed for today’s work. She can decide which negative to print, give the final sheet away or keep the shutter closed. He can surrender his entry and still disagree with her. If they never become close, the film still has a question about authorship. **The shot** is an earlier version of his hand becoming visible in the developing tray. Render from an actual logged camera pose and prop state. A black-and-white room, three silhouettes and a single developing effect are sufficient. The main production risk is that a tiny photographic detail will be illegible in a three-minute cut.

## 3. No Forwarding Address

On the last morning before a mountain post office closes, a relief clerk keeps rediscovering an envelope addressed in his own hand. The postmistress has applied for a mainland job and is trying to decide which parts of her life she wants forwarded. A bus driver will carry one final sack but has a timetable and passengers of their own.

Each character controls what they send. Addresses are visible. Sealed letters convey their contents only if their owners choose to share them. The clerk gets one outgoing place for his own transfer papers. The price of helping somebody is giving up that place or doing the delivery himself. A learned relationship might make him offer; consideration may make him return a letter unopened. The forgotten self’s envelope can turn out to be routine, generous or embarrassingly self-serving. **The shot** is the clerk recognizing his penmanship and sliding the envelope back across the counter, still sealed. This is the cheapest physical set and the clearest version of the twelve-word note. The risk is a film full of people silently reading. Make carriage, stamping and the departing bus visible.

## 4. The Final Dress Rehearsal

A community theater keeps resetting at the same missed lighting cue. The understudy is ready to play the lead, while the woman with the original role would rather call the show from the wings. The stage manager expects a full house and has one rehearsal left. The protagonist keeps finding earlier blocking diagrams with corrections in his own hand.

Working together creates a skill that can outlast fact memory. He can catch her tempo in a new passage even when he cannot recall how they practiced. Her choice may be to perform, direct, give the role away or walk out. The final fork can test whether he keeps cueing the part she once wanted after she has chosen a different one. **The shot** is a spotlight held on the wings while she decides where to stand. A stronger stretch experiment learns timing error on unseen rhythms and tests whether joint practice generalizes to another partner. Timing alone measures coordination. The emotional evidence is what he does when her new wish conflicts with the role they perfected together. This premise needs better character animation than the other three.

## 5. Table Nine

An entire town fits into a hotel ballroom for a winter wedding. The loop begins when a speech goes on too long and the caterer drops a tray. The recurring guest sits beside the bride’s estranged sister. Their tablemate is an aunt who would dearly like to repair the family and, inconveniently, has a microphone. Nobody has to leave town or save an institution. The cast can eat, change seats, dance, enlist the aunt or slip out through the kitchen.

On one pass the sister may tell him why she came. On the next he has to decide what to do with an intimacy she has never granted *this* version of their acquaintance. Breaking confidence could win the room, reconcile the sisters and still hurt the person whose story he told. A quieter ending is the guest moving his chair so the aunt cannot corner her at the table, then sitting somewhere else when she asks. Let her seek the microphone herself on a different run. The place card with pencil marks on the back carries the forgotten self’s handwriting. **The shot** is two chairs pushed together on one night and deliberately moved apart on the next. This is the best option for an ensemble comedy or a romance built out of conversation. It demands more of the dialogue model and the animator than the radio play.

The island still offers the clearest wide shot and the simplest visual departure. The station has the strongest sound design and the most natural reason for an exact repeated sentence. The portrait booth has the strongest silent reveal. The post office gives a two-person team the best chance of finishing. The theater offers the richest learned skill if there is more than a day to build it. Table Nine gives the characters the most freedom to surprise the writers socially.

## Can the actors forget *Groundhog Day*?

There are three distinct interventions. Erasing an actor’s diary is a database operation. Keeping the actor from treating cinematic conventions as evidence is a perception and evaluation problem. Removing knowledge of one movie from model parameters is a machine-unlearning research problem. For a festival set that resembles the film, I would spend the event budget on the second intervention.

**A small direct check changed my view of the risk.** Given the same bare description of the premise at six sampling seeds, the local 4B model got the title right **zero** times. When explicitly given the title and asked whether the movie established a mechanical exit rule, all six replies asserted a rule, with several incompatible explanations. In 18 in-character continuations across three cue levels, none named the film. One used the town name under the strongest Pennsylvania/groundhog cue. These are short responses from one model and three fixed scene prompts. Correct recognition could still appear with another paraphrase, a stronger model or a longer dialogue. The immediate failure was confidently inventing rules from suggestion. ### The event-day experiment

- **Before choosing a model, test both knowledge and use.** In isolated contexts, probe the title by several independent paraphrases, character names, reverse clues and open-ended continuations. Include adjacent comedies and ordinary reasoning about repeated observations as retention controls. Separately score in-character scenes for explicit film references, unsupported film-specific facts and action justified by a copied moral exit rule. A sensible test such as predicting the next dropped spoon is allowed. Blind the evaluator to model, condition and outcome. Cluster results by scenario, with repeated seeds nested inside. Keep a set of paraphrases completely out of development.
- **Give the language actor a world it can investigate.** Its prompt contains current perception, plausible affordances and a few retrieved observations. Omit the project title, file paths, trope tags and the screenplay critic’s vocabulary from actor-visible metadata. Never prompt “forget Groundhog Day” because the instruction supplies the association. Let a character make a wrong theory. Give it an inexpensive chance to predict a specific event, then let the world confirm or disconfirm it. The engine alone can turn Tuesday into Wednesday.
- **Make alternative explanations genuinely different.** Run research-only worlds where the clock, the broadcast reel or a physical contact controls the reset. Change the names and the date independently of the mechanism. If the actor always predicts that personal improvement ends the day, it is using a genre prior where evidence should decide. If its predictions update after a failed test, it can do the job even with the film in its weights. Hold the rules fixed within any canonical film.
- **Preserve bad takes in the audit.** If a character quotes a film or invents an escape, the raw event remains available. A stricter in-period voice or a different model is a new configuration with a new run. A hidden postprocessor that strips references from committed lines would make emergence harder to assess.

### If weight-level unlearning becomes a separate research objective

Use open weights with a measurable baseline on the film. Build independently worded fact and association questions from lawful summaries, plus held-out paraphrases and retention material covering winter festivals, adjacent films, weather reporting, social learning and generic time-loop reasoning. Train a reversible low-rank adapter to lower the likelihood of film-specific answers on the forget set, with a KL penalty to the base model on the retain set. Keep a second adapter trained only on in-character alternatives as a behavioral-steering control. Compare both to the frozen base and a context-only actor. Score held-out question accuracy, plausible-wrong substitution, spontaneous intrusion in full scenes, retained utility and candidate-action changes at matched checkpoints. A model that formerly failed every baseline question provides no measurable forgetting on that instrument.

Publish the narrow effect supported by the tests, such as a reduction in measured film references at a specified retained task score. Disabling the adapter restores the base. A drop in naming accuracy can reflect inhibited recall, general degradation or substitution with a confidently invented answer. The early Harry Potter unlearning work and its subsequent evaluations illustrate how much the conclusion can change with the probe. Even comprehensive finite testing cannot certify that every trace of a work has disappeared from a pretrained network. [Eldan & Russinovich](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02238) · [Eight Methods to Evaluate Robust Unlearning in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16835) · [MUSE benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06460)

Set the story in 1982 because the analogue tools make memory beautiful on camera. The release date of the original movie tells us what the fictional characters could have seen. The model’s pretraining history is a separate fact.
