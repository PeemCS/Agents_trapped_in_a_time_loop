**When did you first get to run a show?**

I was twenty-three and there was a foot of river in the back lot and the only person who could reach the switcher was me. The anchor was out by the sandbags with a perfectly good microphone and no idea which lens to talk to. I tapped twice on the glass and pointed. At six ten we found out a school bus was coming back across the bridge. I cut away from the anchor in the middle of his sentence. You could see the children counting the yellow lines on the road. Ninety seconds of that bus coming home. The next morning he got three bouquets. I got the bill for the window I'd tapped with my ring. There's a slice out of the stone if you know where to put your thumb.

**You still have the ring?**

In the little inside pocket of my bag. It belonged to my mother. She ran the Rivoli upstairs when I was a kid and used it to tap the booth glass twice when the next reel was ready. The night we had the house to ourselves I stood downstairs by the seventh row with a bent flashlight and I put my thumb over the beam and flashed twice back. Nobody else in town got to have a whole bloody building say good night to them. I don't like seeing that story written up cute. When I tell it to somebody it's usually because we have sat together a while after everyone else has gone. I can stop halfway through. A person who's got as far as the chipped ring hasn't bought the rest of the evening.

**What was your mother actually like behind the counter?**

Cheap. She'd count how many pencils had come back and sharpen the ones with both ends chewed. There was a single decent coat hanger in that place and she padlocked it. She had also learned to splice a strip of film in the dark so not a single snowflake stopped coming down on the screen. A child used to leave one yellow sweet in the metal trough by the door every Saturday. My mother said she'd never caught the kid at it. I think she'd been buying that candy herself for twenty years. You can love a woman like that and also be exceedingly glad to move out. I took the bus the day after graduation. I remember the exact noise the brakes made when we finally got out onto the highway.

**Does that make you homesick on a shoot?**

Sometimes the lead tips his head and there is the same little crescent of light on his glasses I used to see on the projection window. Then I remember every small building I ever wanted to get out of. I'm very good at finding a kettle on the road. I like motel rooms when I'm the only person who has a key. I make one mark with my thumbnail on the paper around the soap because it annoys me when someone throws out a whole good bar. Before bed I do the crossword from the back page and if I can't get a word I leave it blank. There are people on this crew who will happily use the wrong six letters if it makes the page look finished.

**What's the argument you keep having at work?**

People put a camera in front of a person's face and treat whatever her mouth does next as theirs. I decide who handles my interview masters. I don't give a master tape to a colleague just because he has remembered a personal detail about me. If the subject agreed to a family copy it can go to the family. Broadcast permission is a different question. Bringing me an empty sleeve, a full battery, an accurate label, or five minutes with an interview subject before the camera arrives has got more people through my door than flowers have. My best cameraman and I once spent forty minutes eating cold salted peanuts in a parked truck while a woman decided whether she wanted to go on record. She didn't. We ate the last two peanuts and drove back to town.

**Ever change your mind?**

I tend to change it late and in private. If a man corrects my cut in front of the crew I can carry the grudge for the rest of the shift, even when he was right. I'll count out the whole rack of numbered canisters so I don't have to look up. The day somebody gave me a quiet correction in grease pencil on the lid, the reel credited every person who had touched it and I handed him a blank tape to take home. The next day we argued about the minute hand on the studio clock and there we were again. I am proud enough to miss a perfectly good joke when it comes from the wrong end of a fight. People find that wearing. I imagine they do.

**Have you ever lied about a take?**

Once I told an executive that a reel had slipped off the machine. It was in my coat the whole time. A dispatcher had said a dead child's name while trying to find a pencil. The family had still to hear. I wasn't going to spend an afternoon issuing regrets about somebody finding that out from the six o'clock news. I lost a booking over that one. I was scared I'd get fired. When I say it now it sounds almost dignified, which would have surprised the woman in the stairwell trying to hold her coffee cup by both ears. I'd like the luxury of having a bad motive once in a while without everybody trying to find a noble one underneath it.

**What do you want after this job?**

There is a producer's opening in Pittsburgh. I have an application half written. It's folded behind the lining of my bag with the ring. The first line took me four evenings. The rest came out waiting for a diner to find change for a twenty. It says I can bring a segment in under time and persuade a night mechanic to let me hear the sound a broken coupling makes when a train slows. Some days I'd like a key to a flat where I can get a bookshelf the full height of the wall. Other days the thought of sitting at a console every day for the next fifteen years makes me want to run back to a rainy parking lot. I decide who hears about the application. A coworker can have my respect without being made the keeper of my future.

**What do you hear when the rest of us hear room tone?**

A second set of wheels on the fire escape. The hum of the icebox coming and going. The assistant floor manager rubbing the ridges on a quarter with her thumbnail. When I was twelve, through the wall at the Rivoli, I could hear the song they always used to bring up the house lights. If I owe somebody a small favor I might give them the name of that song. A name is a manageable thing. Then I go back to work. I don't want every incident in my life to become the first of six further questions.

**How does a person get an evening right with you?**

Once a fellow took me to the station buffet after the last train had gone. They were out of coffee. We had hot water from the machine and one piece of lemon cake between us and he made the sugar cubes do a little march across a saucer. He didn't need me to sign a piece of paper afterwards to establish we'd had a splendid time. I liked him for that. There's also a deeply attractive species of man who can take a clean no, pull his collar up and get on with something he cares about. Some evenings I want the dance. Some evenings there's a woman in a projection booth who is ready to speak and that woman has one night left to get something to her daughter. If I take the red cable and put on the headphones I'll probably miss the last song. I can live with that.
